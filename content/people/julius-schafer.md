++**UPDATE! ++**
Julius Matthias Schäfer was born on October 22, 1908 in Düsseldorf, Germany. His mother was a housewife and his father was a butcher. He attended school until the age of 16 and did not perform compulsory military service. He had an older brother who died in the war in 1942. From 1923 to 1933, Julius was a member of the Nerotherbund, a male scouting organization similar to the Boy Scouts. Julius was the leader of the Order of the Pirates and as Head of the Order in the Lower Rhine district.

After 1933, the youth movement was declared illegal, and Julius became one of the members of the Rhineland Youth League, the Rheinischer Jugendbund. During these years, from 1923 to 1935, Julius changed jobs several times and traveled extensively abroad, traveling to Turkey, Africa, Spain, Greece, and Iceland. These trips were organized by the youth association of which he was one of the leader.

This information is important for understanding Julius’s culture and mindset: the youth movement to which he belonged was naturalistic, altruistic, and cosmopolitan, and openly opposed to Nazi ideology (for example, Julius took part in a strike by left-wing workers in Düsseldorf during the Nazi regime).

Julius’s arrest is connected to many other arrests within the youth organization to which he belonged. On February 13, 1936, all the leaders of the youth movement were arrested (the national leader, Robert Oelbermann, would later die in a Nazi concentration camp). On July 9th, 1936, Julius was sentenced to seven months in prison (this sentence included the period of pre-trial detention that had begun on February 13; in fact, he was released on September 15).

However, on September 22nd, 1936, he was arrested again (without any evidence!) because the Gestapo believed that Julius might reoffend, that is, continue to have sexual relations with other men. This time, Julius was not kept in prison but was transferred first to the Lichtenburg concentration camp and then to the Sachsenhausen concentration camp (admission: October 19th,1936, prisoner number 1000, Block 8 – release: January 21st, 1937).

The concentration camp experience was terrible. A friend of Julius later said:
***“Julius had called me asking to meet; I immediately said: what are you waiting for? Come! When I saw Julla, I didn’t recognize him, he was so changed, so different. He didn’t smile anymore. The concentration camp had broken him.”***

In October 1941, Julius was accused of belonging to an illegal German youth movement, but the charges were dropped and he was acquitted. In 1942, he became engaged to a younger woman. In 1944, he became the father of a child.

The last information about Julius is a letter sent to his wife in June 1944. Also in June, the German army declared him missing near Vitebsk, in Belarus, during Operation Bagration. Unfortunately, his body was never found. I am sure that you, too, have understood the extraordinary and courageous life of Julius, known as Julla. There is much more to tell: he sang, played the guitar, and wrote songs. He was a very intelligent person. In Düsseldorf prison, it was he who came up with a way to communicate with the adjacent cell (they used the hollow metal tubes of the beds: when one spoke, the other would put an ear to the tube; the hollow cavity allowed the sound of the voice to pass through). Likewise, in the concentration camp, it was he who managed to make a pencil and to find paper for the group leader, Robert Oelbermann. He was a free-spirited young man and loved traveling abroad. He never claimed to be innocent of the charges of homosexuality, but he did contest the dates and the circumstances (in the interrogation records he states that he never had complete sexual relations).

I do not believe that other important documents are available; otherwise, I would have found them (my research began in May). I have seen that some people on Instagram have written that Julius was Jewish, but this is not true, because he was Catholic (religion is not important, but lying is wrong).
